# Labs Must Earn Back Trillions, So Don't Build on Their Quicksand

*At AI Engineer NYC 2026, Nous Research CEO Dillon Rolnick argued that AI labs facing commoditized models will either lock customers in or compete with them directly, and urged businesses to build their agents on open foundations such as Hermes Agent.*

**Featured Keynote · Tuesday, October 13, 2026 · 5:06–5:24pm · Simons Room (Metropolitan East), Main Stage**
**Speaker:** Dillon Rolnick, CEO, Nous Research

---

Dillon Rolnick gave the last talk of the day on the main stage. "Today we're going to talk about building on foundations and platforms that you can actually own," Rolnick said, and then posed the question the talk was built around: "How do you make back a [trillion]-dollar infrastructure and research bet?" (The captions read "centillion"; Rolnick later spoke repeatedly of a "trillion dollar" investment.) Individual labs had spent hundreds of billions of dollars on ever more sophisticated systems, and "the industry as a whole has spent trillions perhaps."

The official session description was one line: "Modern businesses require open agents."

## 1. The models are commoditizing

"The problem is though that the models have begun to commoditize," Rolnick said. Practitioners know this intuitively: they switch readily between Astra, Fable, Opus, Kimi "without too much regard for who's on the back end," because they want to try the new model. Rolnick pointed to charts circulating on Twitter that week showing the average cost of a token falling while total spending on tokens surged and the infrastructure cost of serving them stayed flat. Where there used to be margin in a very performant model, it is narrowing as competition makes a baseline level of intelligence widely available.

## 2. Two ways to earn it back

If the models commoditize after that much spending, Rolnick said, there are "two pretty obvious ways" to recover it. One is to "find a different layer of the stack to compete at. You go down to the infrastructure or you go up to the user." The other: "you build God, and you build God faster than anyone else, and you monetize God."

![Compete at a different layer, or build god](../slides/10-13-1706-hermes-gives-you-wings-build-on-open-fou/01.jpg)
*"1. Compete at a different layer 2. Build god"*

On the first path, being in front of the user offers stickiness, "and stickiness means margin," which is why the fight has shifted to the agent layer. "Consumers, though, are fickle and they're terrible customers." Businesses spend heavily on tokens and have economically productive uses for them, so the fight at the user layer goes to businesses, and the best position is to be embedded in one: "when you're in a business, it's hard to take you out."

Rolnick then asked the audience to picture their own company two or three years out. How much of the workforce, measured by output, would be agentic? "70% conservatively, probably more." If 70% of a workforce is organized by someone "who may or may not have adverse incentives to you, they can be organized into a strike." A lab that had to earn back a trillion dollars, Rolnick said, would "use your asymmetric leverage and … change the rules of the game."

## 3. If you build God, you point it at the money

On the second path: "If you succeed at building God, what you would do is you would point God at the most lucrative problems in the world. The best shot at earning it back is not to sell it for an ever decreasing margin to others … The best shot is to solve them yourselves."

![The best shot at earning it back](../slides/10-13-1706-hermes-gives-you-wings-build-on-open-fou/02.jpg)
*The slide's version says "point the models"; on stage Rolnick said "point God."*

"Labs think that markets are solvable problems, and that's exactly how they view the financial industry," Rolnick said: as a tractable problem, "perhaps not even a very hard one." The smart labs know they lack the context, know-how and experience in finance to do it alone, so for now they hire investment banking analysts and partner with some of the biggest firms in the world. "My belief is that they will eventually go to the source because … that's where the money is."

Trapping customers and then raising prices is nothing new, Rolnick acknowledged. "But the difference is that the stakes are much higher." "We're talking about meaningful … percentages of the Earth's economy."

## 4. The choice of foundation will harden

"You in this room need AI," Rolnick said. Everyone has seen what agentic systems can do, "and there's no going back." The vice presidents, executives and engineers in the audience would have to decide now where to "grow roots" around tools and processes. Whatever they picked would harden: the organization would build know-how, processes and systems around it.

"I implore you, do not build on quicksand foundations. Build on open foundations. Build on Hermes Agent."

## 5. Why Nous is making the pitch

"I just spent a fair amount of time convincing you hopefully that the highest expected value move in AI right now is to either trap you and jack up the prices or replace you directly," Rolnick said. "So, why am I standing here?"

Rolnick's answer was that Nous Research was built "primarily for moral and philosophical reasons." In business, morality and incentives often diverge, "and when that happens, it's probably easiest to change your morality." Nous instead believes the best course is one where it and its users are aligned. The company was founded to counter "a seemingly inexorable trend towards centralization and centralized control" of what Rolnick called perhaps the world's most important technology. "Nous Research was built by and staffed by nerds. Nerds who love science fiction," Rolnick said. "Skynet was, after all, a closed source company."

## 6. Software no longer has a moat

"There's no longer really a strong moat in proprietary software. The marginal cost to reproduce a piece of software has plummeted." Rolnick's example: a few months earlier xAI released Grok Bot, and Nous liked its interface. "So we pointed Hermes Agent at it and we reproduced it in a weekend and released it as a feature within Hermes Agent, open source of course." (A trade report in August described Nous publicly testing a "Bot Mode" plugin for Hermes Agent whose design followed Grok Bot; the weekend timeline comes only from the talk.)

Software used to be hard to produce, Rolnick said: coordinated teams spent long hours combining open-source libraries into proprietary products sold as SaaS. "But now the opposite is true. Anything can be reverse engineered," and many motivated open-source builders are pointing their agents at commercial software. "The moats of yesterday were in proprietary IP. The moats of tomorrow are in network effects, reputation, and reliability."

## 7. The same is true of models

Labs, Rolnick said, have tended to underestimate their own tools, or the users of those tools. On the model side, "models can fairly easily teach another model to be nearly as smart as the original model," and a model's advantage rarely lies in its architecture: "it's usually in the quality of the data and the scale."

After a pause, Rolnick described how labs responded. "They labeled the teaching process distillation, and they labeled illicit distillation theft. You cannot call that copyright infringement, because copyright infringement would imply that you somehow took every living human being's written or creative work and then trained on top of it." And "they also trained the models to not assist in the process of training other models, and they labeled that safety." Rolnick named no lab.

## 8. Agents are operating systems, not chatbots

"Agents are like operating systems and they're like frameworks, and they're a lot more like those than they are like chatbots," Rolnick said. Treating an agent as a personal assistant means you "will forever live on the surface."

Rolnick referred back to an earlier main-stage talk that day about how a financial firm turns data sources, routine code, people's opinions and judgment into an investment decision. (Rolnick did not name it; by the livestream's running order it was most likely Coatue's "A Hedge Fund as a Function.") "Agents are really at their heart a system that combines determinism and non-determinism." The new application no longer has to be "cold hard code"; it can combine intelligence, reasoning and judgment.

Companies should build those systems "on foundations that you own," Rolnick said, able to "swap in and out the models that you want" and stack functionality on primitives of code, data sources, judgment and reasoning. "But you should not be spending your entire lives being intelligence companies." Most companies are not agent or AI companies and should focus on what made them successful. When the state of the art moves, "you should inherit that, not rebuild it and maintain it."

"Remember, these are tools that should make you better. They are not drop-ins to replace you," Rolnick said. "Do you know how much energy it takes to create a human?"

## 9. The pitch for Hermes Agent

Hermes Agent can be run "however you want, wherever you want. Modify it as you see fit. If you're not having fun anymore, you can take your ball and you can go home." The moat, Rolnick said, "is no longer entrapping you. The moat is in reputation."

Rolnick's goal is a standard. "Hermes Agent today is the most used and downloaded open source agent in the world," Rolnick said, "order[s] of magnitude more downloaded than some of its closed competitors" (claims for which no independent figures were found). As long as one party is willing to open-source these tools, "water will continue to flow downhill. And I want to be downhill so that I can work with you to provide support, safety, scalability to go along with the world's best open agent."

Rolnick closed: "Somebody is going to earn back the trillion dollar investments that we've made. Make sure that it's you. … You can have state-of-the-art features and reliability without sacrificing your autonomy. Build on something you own. Build with us."
